\begin{abstract}
Visual assembly requires an agent to ground instructions, execute legal
placements, and recover when earlier work changes. Evaluation can confound
these abilities when it treats one reference build order as the only valid
trajectory or waits for its exact intermediate state before introducing a
fault. We introduce \ours, an executable benchmark framework for static
repair and continuous visual assembly under hidden interventions. Its
instruction-guided suite preserves twelve community-designed objects,
provides complete manuals for the remaining 5--12 parts, and contains
96 static and dynamic cases. The agent observes the actual scene after
each action; completion depends on legal execution and terminal geometric
equivalence, while interventions are selected from the actual installed
state. We also document a 3,292-question static geometry bank and a
1,872-episode mechanism bank, retaining their distinct protocols and
validation status. A deterministic study on two legal routes per community
object finds that exact-reference triggers cover only 9/36 alternative-route
fault trials, compared with 36/36 for actual-state interventions. These are
executor diagnostics, not learned-model results. The benchmark makes
construction, intervention exposure, and completed recovery separately
measurable, with source-linked geometry, image, and replay evidence.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Consider a nearly completed toy car. An agent installs the left side of
the cabin before the right side; another agent reverses that order. Both
may be correct. If an evaluator expects the first sequence, a legitimate
intermediate state can be mistaken for an assembly error. A related
problem arises when a hidden fault is scheduled only after the agent
reproduces one reference prefix: a correct alternative route may never
encounter the fault at all. Final success, exposure to a fault, and
adherence to a demonstration are different properties.

The visual specification creates a second ambiguity. A single final
image may conceal joints or internal parts, making reconstruction
underdetermined even for a person. A failed reconstruction then says
little about error monitoring. Our primary task supplies the surrounding
object, a fixed goal, and an accessible manual for a small remaining
module. The manual specifies the pieces and their intended assembly,
but serves as a reference rather than a compulsory trajectory.
Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} shows the twelve objects in this suite.

We study two complementary tasks. In \emph{static repair}, the agent
receives a faulty state and an explicit acceptance target. In
\emph{dynamic completion}, it begins with a preassembled context,
constructs the remaining module, observes an unannounced change to
previous work, and continues toward the original target without being
restarted. Both expose one-brick attach and detach actions, uniform
inspection tools, and fresh visual observations. Their shared principle
is that accepted operations must be executable and the terminal object
must be correct; intermediate agreement with a chosen reference sequence
is not a success criterion.

Prior work provides important components of this setting: physically
checked generation~\cite{pun2025brickgpt,kulits2026bricknet}, visual
manual execution~\cite{wang2022mepnet}, single-step error
correction~\cite{wan2024scanet}, and interactive structural
understanding~\cite{walsman2022breakmake}. Recent agentic design and
robot-recovery benchmarks further broaden the evaluation of construction
and recovery~\cite{kulits2026brickbench,li2026roborecover}. Our focus is
the evaluation contract connecting a visual history, legal alternative
assembly orders, and hidden changes within a continuing episode.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
We make three contributions. (1) We define a visual assembly and repair
protocol that accepts equivalent terminal structures, selects feasible
interventions from the actual state, and separates completion from
intervention exposure. (2) We construct a source-preserving,
instruction-guided suite with 12 objects and 96 cases, alongside audited
static and mechanism banks whose different scopes remain explicit.
(3) We provide reproducible route-sensitivity diagnostics, reference
replays, physical-action rejection checks, and image provenance.
Our present experiments establish properties of the benchmark and
executor. They do not establish a population-level ranking of visual
agents or independently validated human performance.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Generating and understanding brick assemblies.}
BrickGPT generates buildable structures with validity filtering and
physics-aware rollback~\cite{pun2025brickgpt}. BrickNet represents a
larger part vocabulary through connector graphs and learns assembly
sequences from human-designed LDraw objects~\cite{kulits2026bricknet}.
BrickBench evaluates agentic text-conditioned design through validity,
alignment, and design criteria~\cite{kulits2026brickbench}.
These tasks motivate recognizable objects and executable checks; our
agent instead receives a fixed object specification and must restore
or complete it. We reuse the available brick geometry and connector
infrastructure and do not claim a new collision or force simulator.

Break and Make evaluates interactive inspection, disassembly, and
subsequent reconstruction~\cite{walsman2022breakmake}. LEGO-Puzzles
studies elementary spatial reasoning and multistep planning, while
PhyBlock evaluates physical understanding and block assembly
planning~\cite{tang2025legopuzzles,ma2025phyblock}. Our module task
reduces ambiguity about unseen structure by supplying reference
instructions, then introduces a change during the agent's own progress.

\paragraph{Manual execution and error recovery.}
MEPNet translates visual manuals into machine-executable assembly
plans~\cite{wang2022mepnet}. LEGO-ECA and SCANet pair assembly-step
manual images with failures and corrections~\cite{wan2024scanet}.
They are particularly close to our static task. Our dynamic condition
retains a single agent's continuing history before and after an external
change, with opportunities to inspect, repair, and finish.

AsgardBench evaluates visually grounded plan adaptation with lightweight
feedback~\cite{tupini2026asgard}; RoboRecover evaluates policies from
reconstructed execution-deviation states~\cite{li2026roborecover}.
Thus dynamic recovery itself is not new. The distinction here is the
combination of instruction-assisted brick modules, one-part geometric
execution, and an explicit test of how reference-order assumptions
change which trajectories are evaluated.

\section{Task and Execution Contract}
\subsection{State, observations, and actions}
A task consists of an immutable context $B$, movable inventory $P$,
candidate poses $C_p$ for each part $p$, an initial state $s_0$, and an
acceptance target $g$. The state maps each movable part to an installed
pose or the rack: $s(p)\in C_p\cup\{\bot\}$. The context is already
assembled and cannot be removed. In the legacy static and mechanism
banks, the environment includes a white baseplate. The community module
suite additionally preserves the original surrounding object as a rigid
fixture. We do not equate completing that module with building the
entire source object from an empty table.

At decision $t$, the ordinary visual observation contains the fixed
acceptance target, initial context, available manual, inventory, the
previous scene, and the actual current scene (Fig.~\ref{fig:protocol}).
Three fixed views---isometric, rear, and top---remain available.
The first observation explicitly lacks a previous action. Public part
and placement handles are randomized; neither their names nor image
filenames encode a goal label. An inspection can request another view,
a part card, or a candidate-placement preview. A preview depicts a
hypothetical placement and does not change the world or certify that
placement as correct.

The action space is
\begin{equation}
 a_t\in\{\operatorname{attach}(p,c),\operatorname{detach}(p),
          \operatorname{inspect}(q),\operatorname{finish}\}.
\end{equation}
One manipulation moves one brick. The executor checks pose validity,
endpoint collision, a compatible connection for attachment, and a
collision-free straight insertion or extraction path. Rejected actions
leave the state unchanged and return a legality code. These codes can
assist recovery; they are recorded separately from visual observations.
The module manipulation budget is $4|P_g|+12$, where $P_g$ denotes the
normal target pieces; inspections are counted separately.

\subsection{Completion without a prescribed trajectory}
Let $\sig(s)$ be the multiset of installed part geometries, colors, and
poses, accounting for explicitly supported symmetries of plain
rectangular pieces. Completion is
\begin{equation}
 C(\tau)=\mathbf{1}[a_T=\operatorname{finish}]
          \mathbf{1}[\sig(s_T)=\sig(g)].
 \label{eq:completion}
\end{equation}
All preceding accepted actions must pass the executor. Swapping
indistinguishable pieces does not change the terminal signature.
Candidates include interchangeable placements for matching pieces.
A floating brick, intersecting placement, duplicate attachment, or
state replacement cannot be accepted as a shortcut.

A manual $M=(\bar a_1,\ldots,\bar a_n)$ supplies one verified build
witness and images explaining each part's intended placement
(Fig.~\ref{fig:instructions}). Its intermediate states $\bar s_k$ are
not constraints on $s_t$. Figure~\ref{fig:orders} gives a concrete
counterexample to equating equal progress with equal geometry. Static
repair uses the same terminal rule, but its acceptance target is the
explicit stage image supplied with that question, rather than the
completed module. The stage index identifies that target image; it does
not constrain the agent's repair sequence.

\subsection{Hidden interventions on the actual state}
After an accepted manipulation, let $\tilde s_{t+1}$ be the executor's
result. A condition specifies an error family $f$ and minimum installed
count $k$. The event selector searches for an edit $e$ when at least $k$
normal inventory pieces are installed. A valid edit must (i) change the
actual geometry or color, (ii) consist of legal physical primitives,
and (iii) have a legal inverse restoring the pre-event state. For removal
and color replacement, it selects a previously installed part and retains
the newest part. An addition uses a compatible exposed connection.
Candidates are considered deterministically given the trajectory.
\begin{equation}
 s_{t+1}=\begin{cases}
 e(\tilde s_{t+1}) & \text{if a feasible, unused event is selected},\\
 \tilde s_{t+1} & \text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:event}
\end{equation}
The selector neither compares $\tilde s_{t+1}$ with a reference prefix
nor asks whether the agent installed a particular set of target pieces.
If no feasible edit exists, the event is deferred. At most one event is
applied in the current module suite. A color replacement comprises two
legal environment primitives, detach then attach, executed between agent
observations. The agent still receives only one physical move per action.

The inventory and candidate catalog are identical across an object's
control and fault conditions. Each normal piece has a same-shape
alternative-color spare, and one extra red plate is available, giving
$2|P_g|+1$ inventory pieces. A different legal order can produce a
different feasible intervention location. Accordingly, \emph{completion
scoring} is independent of the chosen reference order, while
\emph{intervention selection} is adaptive and need not yield identical
fault difficulty across trajectories.

\section{Benchmark Construction}
\subsection{Three evidence scopes}
Table~\ref{tab:banks} separates the banks rather than adding their model
counts into a purported collection of independent objects. They share
rigid brick manipulation infrastructure but differ in task initialization,
instructions, visual admission, and experimental history.

\paragraph{Instruction-guided community modules.}
We select twelve original LDraw OMR scenes and preserve all source
instances, author metadata, original files, and source transforms.
The task removes a small connected-to-context module from each scene;
the surrounding object is supplied as a fixture. The original scenes
contain 52--556 instances, including figures and scenery, while the
remaining tasks contain only 5--12 parts, totaling 99 normal placements.
A complete-object view establishes what is being built; fixed module
views make the remaining placement and errors visible.

For each module we construct a no-error condition and three error
families: one missing part, a same-shape color replacement, and an extra
plate. Each is instantiated as static repair and dynamic completion,
yielding $12\times4\times2=96$ cases. Static targets and fault snapshots
are paired with the illustrated reference route's dynamic checkpoint.
This pairing does not imply that every alternative route encounters the
same affected brick. Figure~\ref{fig:errors} shows three such snapshots.
The supplement enumerates all objects, modules, and intervention counts.

\paragraph{Static geometry bank.}
The white-baseplate bank contains 3,292 questions across 160 model
configurations. Its migration ledger records 2,144 directly replayable
plans, 834 newly verified dismantle/rebuild witnesses, 37 witnesses using
held components, and ten corrected initial scenes that had penetrated
the board. Another 267 incompatible source cases were replaced with
distinct witnessed repair states, preserving replacement provenance.
All 3,292 final witnesses replay under the new rules. This establishes
geometric executability; the complete RGB package and per-question
human readability review remain unfinished.

\paragraph{Dynamic mechanism bank.}
The broader bank contains 1,872 episodes on 68 structures, including
42 recognizable simplified objects. There are 160 normal controls and
1,712 interventions in twelve families. These cover removals, shifts,
rotations, swaps, color/size/type substitutions, added layers, early
covers, buried color errors, cascaded removals, and compound faults.
Complete-object tasks use 6--15 parts. Their templates and evaluation
history are versioned separately from the new community modules;
existing template locations do not acquire new visual validation merely
because an adaptive event is executable.

\subsection{Geometry and visibility admission}
Every operated part must have usable collision geometry. Each installation,
intervention primitive, and reference recovery is checked against the
present assembly and environment. Unsupported background collision
meshes in community scenes are retained visually and represented by
conservative bounding boxes for endpoint and swept-path rejection;
they are never silently omitted as obstacles. The context's internal
construction history, force stability, and grasp mechanics are outside
the task's claims.

The module renderer uses the same camera and framing for current,
reference, and fault states. In each module, every manual addition has
at least 128 visible pixels in one of the three 900-by-660 interface
views; each reference fault changes at least 128 pixels by at least
24 RGB intensity levels in one view. These are reproducible screening
thresholds, not perceptual guarantees. They apply to the checked
reference scenes; an adaptive event at another location needs its own
visibility record before supporting a visual-miss claim.

The mechanism bank separately records 1,553 primary temporal candidates,
136 events that cancel the latest action, and 23 low-contrast cases.
Cancellation matters: if a new part is immediately removed, the scene
before the action and the next current scene may look identical.
That tests action-outcome monitoring and should not be silently pooled
with changes to older completed work.

\section{Evaluation and Diagnostic Experiments}
\subsection{Completion, exposure, and recovery}
For a scheduled fault trial $i$, let $T_i$ indicate an actual intervention
and $C_i$ the completion criterion in Eq.~\ref{eq:completion}. We report
\begin{equation}
 A_{\rm complete}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i C_i,\qquad
 A_{\rm trigger}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i T_i,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 A_{\rm recover}=\frac{\sum_i T_iC_i}{\sum_i T_i}.
 \label{eq:recover}
\end{equation}
An untriggered but correctly completed episode counts as completion,
not as a successful repair or a completion failure. Empty conditional
denominators remain undefined. A control false alarm, invalid output,
rejected manipulation, or service failure is recorded as its own outcome.
Inspection count and physical action count are also reported; the stored
recovery witness is feasible and is not claimed to be globally optimal.

A final correct object establishes effective recovery after exposure,
but does not by itself establish visual detection. A detection study
requires a separate preregistered judgment or a trace criterion with
clear timing relative to legality feedback. We therefore avoid labeling
every incomplete episode a visual miss. To compare recovery causally,
a paired evaluation branches a saved agent/environment prefix into
control and intervention continuations, retaining the same preceding
observations and actions. Independently generated normal trajectories
are useful construction controls but are not matched-prefix evidence.

\paragraph{Aggregation and splits.}
Case variants from one object are dependent. Model comparisons should
report per-source and per-error results, source-macro averages, and
uncertainty intervals clustered by source. The mechanism release has a
43-family development/evaluation split; its balanced subsets contain
108 and 168 intervention cases, respectively. The community suite is
currently a development and protocol-diagnostic collection, not an
unseen-source leaderboard. Overlap between older static and dynamic
sources precludes claiming cross-bank generalization from their names
alone. The supplementary protocol specifies the additional experiments
needed for a learned-agent comparison.

\subsection{Does the trigger depend on one build order?}
We run a deterministic diagnostic over all twelve community modules.
For each, the reference route and one physically verified alternative
route are executed under control, missing-part, wrong-color, and
extra-part conditions. This gives 96 dynamic route--condition trials:
24 controls and 72 fault trials. Alternative routes are deliberately
selected as regression tests, favoring a different physical state at
the intervention threshold when feasible. They are not random samples
from the set of all legal assembly orders.

We compare an \emph{exact-prefix trigger}, which requires equality with
the reference checkpoint, against the actual-state selector. To measure
the former's opportunity without introducing a different repair policy,
we inspect each otherwise normal route for that reference state. Both
schedules cover all 36 reference-route fault trials. On the alternative
routes, exact-prefix eligibility falls to 9/36, whereas actual-state
interventions occur in 36/36 (Table~\ref{tab:orders}). The difference
comes from nine objects whose threshold states differ physically from
the reference. Thus the diagnostic isolates a protocol effect with
identical target objects and legal construction routes; it does not
measure a language model's tendency to choose those routes.

The corresponding intermediate-state acceptance rule rejects all twelve
alternative normal routes, despite their correct endpoints. Terminal
multiset comparison accepts them all. Separate regression checks exchange
identical part identities in each of the twelve modules and preserve
success, while rejecting repeated, floating, and overlapping attachments
and premature completion. These checks exercise both sides of the
contract: freedom to choose a valid sequence does not permit fictitious
physical actions.

\subsection{Are interventions consequential and recoverable?}
A blind continuation policy attempts the reference or alternative normal
route without reading the changed state. A privileged recovery policy
uses the evaluator's feasible inverse after a triggered edit, then
continues that route. The blind policy completes all 24 controls and
none of the 72 fault trials; privileged recovery completes all 96 trials.
These controls show that the selected faults alter the endpoint and
that a legal recovery exists. Neither policy tests visual understanding.
Figure~\ref{fig:recovery} illustrates one such certified recovery.

The broader mechanism bank provides complementary checks: blind
continuation completes 160/160 controls and 0/1,712 intervention episodes;
a privileged reset/rebuild witness completes 1,872/1,872. We report these
within their own bank because event templates, initial states, and
action histories differ from those of the module suite.

\subsection{Empirical evidence boundary}
The current community and mechanism revisions have no completed
independent human operation study and no new paid-model evaluation.
A historical, differently versioned development pilot recorded one
model completing a normal six-part car and two selected intervention
runs. One retained human submission contained eleven unprompted binary
change judgments; two reported agreements lack independent stored
submissions. These observations support limited interface feasibility,
not a success rate for the present banks, inter-rater agreement, or
model ranking. We preserve their version and scope in the
supplement rather than extending their denominators to newly added data.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\paragraph{What the diagnostics establish.}
The order study demonstrates that a reference-prefix prerequisite can
remove intervention opportunities on valid trajectories. The geometric
replays establish legal completions, and the public/private separation
makes target coordinates, event metadata, and recovery witnesses
unavailable through ordinary observations. These are necessary
conditions for evaluating visual repair. They do not prove that a model
failure must be perceptual: instruction clarity, candidate search,
feedback use, and planning remain potential causes.

\paragraph{Coverage and physical abstraction.}
Twelve community objects provide recognizable context, but only three
fault families and one event per module. The larger mechanism bank
adds error diversity through shorter simplified structures. Neither
count establishes performance on long realistic construction projects.
Full scene size and the number of dependent case variants should not
be presented as task difficulty or independent sample size.
The executor uses finite pose catalogs, rigid connectors, and straight
motion witnesses. It does not model finger geometry, friction, elastic
clutch, force stability, or robot reachability. Conservative obstacles
can reject feasible real-world motions; accepted simulated moves are
not demonstrations of physical robot execution.

\paragraph{Visual validity and statistical claims.}
Pixel-change screening cannot replace independent human interpretation
and end-to-end operation. Adaptive fault locations also need fresh
visibility checks; inherited reference-view approval is insufficient.
The remaining empirical requirements are source-disjoint evaluation,
multiple visual models and repeated runs, independent human completion,
and controlled observation ablations. Until those measurements exist,
we make no claim of benchmark saturation, model ranking, or a measured
human--model gap. Community source attribution and licensing accompany
the artifact, and no personally identifying reviewer records are needed
for the released task definitions.

\section{Conclusion}
\ours evaluates static repair and continuing visual assembly with a
fixed target, explicit remaining instructions, and executable physical
actions. Its module protocol permits different legal build orders and
selects hidden edits from actual progress. Across two verified routes
per object, the diagnostic exposes 27 missed trigger opportunities
under an exact-reference prerequisite while preserving correct terminal
completions. Source-preserving modules, separately audited larger banks,
and explicit evidence boundaries provide a reproducible basis for
measuring visual assembly and recovery without confusing a reference
sequence with the set of valid solutions.
